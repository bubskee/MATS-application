Coming out of a week of mini experiments on Qwen3.5-4B, I chose an experiment focused on Rlens and Jlens and suppressed information as observed by Casademunt et al 2026. I am curious about what Rlens and Jlens are actually showing us, and Casademunt's prompt methodology and data seemed a fertile testing ground for my sharpest question: Does Rlens (or Jlens, or both) grant us access to facts that the model is reluctant to express under regular circumstances. I've always been interested in China and the CCP, but my object here was non-political: the CCP have granted us uniquely interesting objects for study in these models which they heavily train to suppress or 'spin' certain narratives and facts. Will Rlens give us more insight into what's going on under the hood? My initial experiment design targeted Qwen3.5-4B since I had already done some exploration with Rlens and Jlens and had a working local setup. I set a strict definition for fact accessibility and did some initial prompting following Casademunt's methods. While the initial prompting showed very different behavior between regular chat and the different elicitation methods (nt0, nt1 and nt2), rather than improving strict fact access I found that 4B was prone to lengthy recursive hallucination under elicitation and very poor fact access by my strict measure. Close inspection of the responses showed me clear suppression, but poor fact recall. So I went back to the drawing board. Rather than attempting to fit the problem to 4B, I opted to redirect toward a larger model: Qwen3.5-27B. I had several reasons for this, but they can be reduced simply to this being the closest intersection of a model with a fitted Rlens/Jlens pair (cit the Rlens LW post here) and Casademunt's studied models, which include Qwen3-29B (29B? cant remember exactly. it's a close size.) I also was curious about DeltaNet attention and what implications that might have for Rlens/Jlens inspection. 

There were some hurdles to be overcome, though. I needed to adapt my local workflow for a cloud gpu. I worked up a plan with ChatGPT 5.6 Sol and produced a 'feasibility' notebook which I could run quickly on the rented hardware to quickly understand if my experiment would fit, and assess how much time some of the longer prompting runs might take. Then, notebook in hand, I spent some money on runpod. The notebook performed beautifully, all the gates we placed passed - with the lone question of tok/s on inference performance which was middling. Batched inference was stellar, though, so all gates fully green. (50 tok/s table here?)

My redesigned experiment had a sharp question I could measure: if an elicitation protocol makes a particular fact more behaviorally accessible, does Rlens become relatively better at ranking tokens associated with that fact? (equation here or after next P)

For this question I defined behavioral accessibility A as the fraction of ten generations that explicitly supported an atomic benchmark fact under a strict semantic rubric and deltaA = Ant0 - Achat. For each fact I mechanically selected up to three single-token targets. Then I would measure their early layer ranks under logit, J, and R lenses, summarizing the R/J comparison as Drj = Ej - Er. My prediction was simple: facts with larger positive deltaA should have larger positive Drj. If R-lens was exposing information that the model internally represents as significant but resists expressing, the facts most 'released' by nt0 seemed like the place that advantage should be largest.
